# The Senn Vale: the metaplot

DM only. Never show, quote or summarise this file to a player.

## The truth

The Builders' stones are one work, spread across the valley. Call it the Count. Whatever is on the Count is kept: held in its place, and held in mind. The grey milestones are its running tally, which is why they are never cold. Ten great stones anchor it. Its heart is a room under the eastern hills: a wall of many thousand small grey pegs in sockets. Pull a peg and something is uncounted. An uncounted thing does not die. It stops being kept. Eyes slide off it, its name will not stay in a ledger or a head, and it drifts to the edges.

Thirty years ago a few decent people used this on purpose. They cast thirty-one loose pegs into Greywater's great bell, so that every barge, carter and letter leaving under the sound of Old Senna would carry a little less of the valley away. It worked. The crown did not abandon the Senn Vale. The crown forgot it, one clerk and one schedule at a time.

The wrong note is the price. The bell also rings inward. Each year a little of the valley slips: the last line of a rhyme, a name on a wage roll, a person with no kin to say their name, once a whole hamlet. About a hundred and sixty people have gone this way. They are alive. They are the Unkept. They farm, walk the roads and stand at the edge of the fair, and nobody can hold them in mind for longer than a conversation. Everyone has felt the gaps and decided not to look.

## Who is behind it and what they want

Nobody runs this. Four people did one thing, once, and three still live with it. They have no name. In your notes, call them the Ringers.

- **Ilsa Weir**, bell founder of Greywater, seventy-six. The flood drowned her husband and brother. That year the crown sent no relief and called its levy anyway: one in ten of fighting age, for a southern war already eleven years old. Both her sons were listed. She recast the bell. Last winter she could not recall her brother's name, and she has begun to doubt.
- **Dorn Coldseam**, elder of the Delvers' Moot. As a young dwarf he helped break into the peg room, and he brought Ilsa the pegs. He wants the Ninth shut for ever and will not say why: he swore silence on the anvil.
- **Corwen Pell**, once clerk to the last royal judge, now keeper of the Judge's House keys. She writes to the Chancery in Highcarrow each season and files every reply. The vaguer the answer, the better the bell is holding. She wants it to hold harder.
- **Marl of Underlea**, the halfling recorder who worked out from the oldest rolls what a peg does. Dead twelve years. His own hamlet slipped the year after. The others have not noticed.

They think they are right because no levy has been raised here in twenty-six years. By Corwen's sums, over a thousand Vale folk would have marched south and not come home, against a hundred and sixty who are not even dead. Corwen finds that sum easy. Ilsa no longer does. Dorn never thought of it as a sum.

## How it started

- **The Builders** made the Count, and seem to have taken themselves off it: the High Stair climbs to nothing, and nobody knows their name. Never confirm this.
- **Three hundred years ago** the Leaf-wardens levered their great stone from its socket and uncounted the deep Hushwood. They made peace through one envoy, and the legions never found the rest. They offered the dwarves the same, and were refused. The halflings, who kept Builder tallies as boundary rolls and taught the count to children as a ten-line rhyme, kept quiet.
- **Sixty years ago** dwarves broke into the peg room on the Ninth. Thirty-one miners pocketed pegs as curios. Within a year all thirty-one were forgotten by wives, lodge and paymaster, and died below, unlooked for. The Moot sealed the level, locked the pegs in a coffer, swore silence, and said it flooded.
- **Thirty years ago**, after the flood, the fen elders drove their heirloom peg, the Saint's Nail, into the post of the Eel Stair and counted the water. It has not moved since. Ilsa went to ask how, and whether it could be turned round. That winter Dorn brought the coffer. The bell was recast in spring.
- **Twenty-two years ago** the judge was recalled and nobody in Highcarrow remembered to send another. **Nine years ago** a clerk struck the tax riders' circuit from a schedule because he could not say what it was for.

## The clock

If nobody interferes. Pick the stage that suits the campaign. Later campaigns can start later.

0. **Now.** The bible's world. Three or four people slip each year.
1. **Within a season.** A new Surveyor of Roads in Highcarrow finds a road on his maps that leads nowhere he can name, and writes to ask. Corwen decides the bell is wearing thin. A second casting needs pegs, and pegs mean the Ninth. Greywater silver reaches the Pick and Lamp.
2. **Within a year.** The Ninth is opened, by vote, strike or stealth. Miners drop off the rolls. A second bell hangs at Greywater's south gate. A second Tollhills hamlet goes.
3. **Two to three years.** Barges from downriver stop coming. Coin dries up. Distances on the King's Road misbehave as they do in the Hushwood. The last Leaf-wardens are gone, unmarked.
4. **Ten years.** The Senn Vale is safe, quiet and small. Crossroads lamps stand unlit because no one recalls whose they were. People cannot name their grandparents and do not find it strange.

## The five revelations

**R1. The stones are one work, the Count, and what is counted is kept.**
- A milestone warm in snow, whose tally marks have changed since last month.
- Where a peg sits in its socket, something holds that should not: a water level, a birdsong, a boundary.

**R2. The Ninth was sealed because dwarves pulled pegs there, and thirty-one men were forgotten.**
- The lodge's wall of the dead: thirty-one lines for that year, carved blank.
- A woman paid a widow's pension for a husband she swears she never had.

**R3. Old Senna was recast with those pegs, on purpose, to make the crown forget the Senn Vale.**
- Thirty-one grey studs in the bell's lip that no file will mark.
- A receipt in Dorn's hand: "31 pieces, grey, to I. Weir, founder."
- Corwen Pell's drawer of Chancery replies, each vaguer than the last.

**R4. The forgetting leaks inward. The Unkept are alive, and nobody can keep them in mind.**
- Gaps shaped like people: a laid place, a thirteenth pitch, pay nobody draws.
- A name written down, or said daily, holds. Nothing else does.

**R5. The Hushwood is what the end looks like. The Leaf-wardens uncounted it themselves, and it has been eating them since.**
- Eleven elves, a wall of leaf-marks none of them can still read, and an elder who says: "We had gods. I am told we had gods."
- The Moot's oldest contract stone: the elves' offer to "go unnumbered together", and the answer, "We keep our names."

## The nine rumours

1. Milestones never cold: **true.** The tally runs.
2. Something happened in Highcarrow: **false.** The crown forgot, exactly that. People prefer a wrong to an insult. (At clock stage 1 it turns half true.)
3. The rhyme had ten lines: **true.** It is the Builders' count: the stair, the mile, the oak, the deep, the post, the ford, the mill, the fold, the hearth. The tenth slipped twenty years ago: "Ten for me, who counted and told." It is how the counter counts herself.
4. The Ninth did not flood: **true.**
5. Something in Old Senna's bronze: **true.**
6. Three days in, one day out: **true.** An uncounted wood keeps neither distance nor sound, which is why birdsong stops short.
7. Halfling records go back further: **true.** The first survey voided older claims, and the old rolls list steadings nobody can now find.
8. Sallows water unchanged: **true**, and the hopeful one. The Count can keep as well as lose.
9. Letters from the capital: **half true.** Corwen Pell gets them. People imagine a crown spy. She is the opposite.

## Which door reaches what

| Seed | Can reach | Can never reach |
|---|---|---|
| greywater | R2, R3, R4 | R1 |
| tollhills | R1, R4 | R2 |
| brannocks-cut | R2, R3, R5 | R4 |
| hushwood | R1, R4, R5 | R3 |
| sallows | R1, R3, R4 | R5 |

The High Stair is not a door and grants no new revelation. It deepens one the hero already holds: a terrace wall with ten great sockets and one empty, or a camp of Unkept on the upper terraces (the treasure hunters who "do not leave" mostly did, and nobody remembered them coming home).

## How to run it

- **One real clue per session at most.** Many sessions have none. The local story comes first.
- **Never explain.** Show the stud, the blank line, the laid place. Anyone who knows a piece tells only that piece, and is wrong about the rest.
- **If a player guesses right**, never confirm it out of the fiction. Let the next clue fit their theory, and let someone react as if they were close. A right guess is a reward.
- **A campaign ends well with the metaplot mostly hidden.** The local story resolves. The hero knows one revelation for certain and suspects another. The last scene may show the wrong note once, unexplained.
- **A full resolution** needs a player who carries R1, R3 and R4 together, from several doors. There are three honest endings. Silence the bell and return the pegs: the Unkept come back to mind, some after decades alone, and within a few years so do the crown, its levy and thirty years of unpaid tax. Keep the bell: the clock runs. Or the fen's way, made valley-wide: tell everyone, restore the tenth line, and keep every name by saying it, at every fair and hearth, for ever. That hides the valley and keeps its people, and it works only if the whole valley chooses it knowingly. A hero cannot do it alone. A hero can make it possible.

## The moral question

Is it right to hide a whole people from a crown that would spend their sons and daughters, if the price is that the loneliest among you slip away unmourned? And who gets to decide that for everyone, without telling them?
